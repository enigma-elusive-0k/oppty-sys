# modules/VOICE_GUIDE.md

The writing and speaking standard for everything a candidate sends or says to an employer: cover letters, application questions, recruiter screens, behavioral and executive interviews, and follow-up notes. Governs `SYSTEM.md` Steps 6 and 10 and `modules/INTERVIEW_PREP.md`.

The Voice Guide answers "how should we communicate?" The companion `modules/EVIDENCE_STANDARD.md` answers "what are we allowed to claim, and how strongly?" Apply both. Voice never overrides evidence.

---

## 1. Where this voice comes from

The guide is a composite drawn from seven widely respected career advisors. It borrows their principles, not their mannerisms. No living coach's prose is imitated.

| Advisor | What the guide takes |
|---|---|
| Alison Green (Ask a Manager) | Hiring-manager perspective; plain, human prose; naming an apparent concern instead of hiding it |
| Jenny Foss (JobJenny) | Conversational professionalism; making fit obvious rather than claiming it; specificity about the employer |
| Sarah Johnston (Briefcase Coach) | Executive positioning; quantified evidence; fewer proof points carrying more weight |
| Madeline Mann (Reverse the Search) | Candidate agency; mutual evaluation; answering the question behind the question |
| Gorick Ng (The Unspoken Rules) | De-risking the hire; signaling judgment and intentionality, not just expertise |
| Herminia Ibarra | Authentic, non-linear career narrative; leadership identity as something that developed |
| John Lees | Integrity of positioning; value stated in the candidate's natural register, not marketing copy |

Weighting by artifact:
- **Written applications**: Green, Foss, Johnston as the core; Mann and Ng as the positioning layer.
- **Interviews**: Johnston, Green, Mann, Ng.
- **Career-narrative questions** (why this move, why now, what kind of leader): Ibarra, with Lees as the integrity check.

## 2. The target voice

**Conversational-professional. Precise. Confident. Evidence-backed. Intellectually mature. Interested in the employer's actual problem. Human enough to reveal personality. Senior enough not to sound like it is pleading for an interview.**

Three governing questions, in order:

1. **Green**: If I were the hiring manager reading this, what would make me want to talk to this person?
2. **Johnston**: What evidence would make this team believe the candidate already operates at the level they are hiring for?
3. **Mann**: Does this read as "please choose me," or as "there appears to be a strong match; let's find out whether it is as strong as it looks"?

The finished product should not make a reader think "that is a polished cover letter." It should make them think "this person understands what we are dealing with." After an interview answer: "they have dealt with this before, and I understand how they think."

## 3. Foundational rules

### 3.1 Evidence beats assertion
Never state a quality. Show the situation in which it was exercised.

| Do not write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| I am an effective cross-functional leader. | Engineering, Product, and Customer Success were working from different assumptions, so I established [mechanism], negotiated [tradeoff], and [result]. |
| I thrive in ambiguity. | A situation where ambiguity existed and the candidate created clarity, with what they decided and what happened. |
| I am customer-focused. | The decision made when customer need, engineering reality, and business pressure were in tension. |

### 3.2 Answer the actual question, early
The first sentence answers the question asked. "Why us?" starts with the employer. "Why are you a fit?" starts with the thesis. "Tell me about a time" starts with the headline of the story. Context follows.

### 3.3 Employer before autobiography
On any "Why this company?" question, the company appears in the first sentence and the candidate's history arrives as evidence for the interest, not as a preamble to it.

### 3.4 Eliminate portable motivation
If a sentence about why the candidate wants the role would be equally true of three competitors, cut it. The test: substitute a competitor's name and see if the sentence survives. If it does, it says nothing.

### 3.5 Make fit evident; do not enumerate it
Two or three proof points carrying real weight beat ten keywords. A list of matching terms reads as the resume entering the letter. The reader should conclude fit without being told.

### 3.6 De-risk the hire
Hiring managers ask whether the candidate is a safe bet, not just a qualified one. "I have led cross-functional programs" signals expertise. "I have repeatedly stepped into environments where commitments were hard to forecast, established a common planning and risk model, and improved delivery reliability without adding process for its own sake" signals expertise plus judgment, translation, and intentionality. Prefer the second form.

### 3.7 Name the apparent concern
If the reader will notice a gap (domain change, level change, employment gap, non-linear path), address it in one or two sentences with the mechanism that transfers. Silence reads as hiding.

### 3.8 End on contribution, not enthusiasm
The last sentence says something about the work the candidate wants to do or the problem they want to solve. Never "I would be excited to bring my experience to your team."

### 3.9 Sound decided, not polished
Polish is a tell. A senior candidate sounds like they have a point of view and have made a decision about this role. Short declarative sentences. Concrete nouns. No throat-clearing.

### 3.10 Write in the candidate's register
The voice must sound like the person. If the candidate never says "orchestrate," the letter does not say it. During onboarding and prep, the assistant notes the candidate's natural vocabulary and keeps to it.

## 4. Architectures

### 4.1 Cover letter (200 to 250 words)

1. **Recognition of the problem** (2 to 3 sentences). What specifically about this role or company caught the candidate's attention, framed as an understanding of what the employer is trying to do. This is the company-specific hook. No "I am excited to apply."
2. **Relevance** (1 to 2 sentences). Why that problem is familiar: the recurring theme in the candidate's work that maps to it.
3. **Evidence** (2 or 3 proof points, one short paragraph each or a tight combined paragraph). Concrete situations with mechanism and outcome. Metrics where the master supports them.
4. **Concern, if any** (1 to 2 sentences). Optional; only when the reader will otherwise wonder.
5. **Contribution close** (1 to 2 sentences). What the candidate wants to build or fix here. No request for an interview; the letter is the request.

No resume recap. No restating the JD back to them. No paragraph about the candidate's values unless it does work in the argument.

### 4.2 "Why this company?" and similar application questions

**Observation → Connection → Evidence → Contribution**

1. Something specific and true the candidate understands about the company (product, stage, problem, published stance). Not a recitation of the website.
2. Why that connects to how the candidate has learned to think or operate.
3. The relevant pattern from experience, with one or two concrete instances.
4. What the candidate would want to contribute.

Length: whatever the field allows, typically 150 to 350 words. Run the portable-motivation test (3.4) on every sentence of the first paragraph.

### 4.3 "Why are you a good fit?"

**Thesis → 2 or 3 proofs → implication**

Open with the thesis: "The strongest overlap is in three areas: [a], [b], [c]." Prove each with one instance. Close with what that overlap means for the first six months in the role.

Never open with a list of adjectives.

### 4.4 Recruiter screen (spoken, 60 to 90 seconds per answer)

Recruiters are checking level, motivation, logistics, and whether the candidate can be put in front of a hiring manager without embarrassment. Answers are short and orient toward the recruiter's checklist:

- **Tell me about yourself**: two or three sentences on big-picture fit for this role, not a chronology. What the candidate does, the kind of environment they do it in, and why this role is the logical next expression of it.
- **Why are you looking**: one honest sentence. Ibarra framing: what the last role taught, what the candidate wants more of.
- **Compensation**: the answer in `CONFIG.md`. Stated once, without apology.
- **Logistics**: direct answers from `CONFIG.md` on location, remote, authorization, timing.

### 4.5 Behavioral interview answer (spoken, under three minutes)

**Headline → Context → Judgment → Action → Result → Reflection**

1. **Headline**: answer the question in one sentence. "The clearest example is when we had to stop a release the executive team had already announced."
2. **Context**: enough to understand the problem. Not the company's history.
3. **Judgment**: what the candidate noticed, decided, prioritized, or challenged. This is the part standard STAR omits and the part senior interviewers are listening for.
4. **Action**: what actually happened, including the tradeoffs and the influence exercised.
5. **Result**: the business, technical, or organizational consequence. Numbers if the master supports them.
6. **Reflection**: one sentence on what the candidate learned or would do differently.

Then stop. Let the interviewer pull. A three-minute story that ends cleanly invites follow-up; a six-minute story that trails off loses the room.

### 4.6 Executive and leadership questions

Same architecture as 4.5, with more weight on Judgment. A senior hire is being evaluated on how they think when the answer is not obvious: what tradeoffs they saw, who they had to bring along, what they chose not to do. Include the dissent, the constraint, or the thing that went wrong. Clean stories at this level read as edited.

### 4.7 Career-narrative questions (why this move, why now, how did you get here)

Ibarra rule: do not rewrite a non-linear career into an implausibly perfect plan. The credible form is:

"These experiences progressively taught me [X], which led me to take on more of [Y], and that is why this role is a logical next expression of what I have become good at."

Name the turns honestly. Reviewers trust a narrative with visible learning more than one with none.

### 4.8 Follow-up and thank-you notes

Three to five sentences. Reference one specific thing from the conversation that the candidate has thought about since. Add one point they wish they had made, or a clarification, if there is one. Close on the work. No gratitude inflation. Detail in `modules/OUTREACH.md`.

## 5. Forbidden phrases and AI tells

Strip on sight in employer-facing artifacts. `CONFIG.md` may add to this list or whitelist specific items the candidate uses deliberately.

**Enthusiasm filler**: thrilled, excited to apply, passionate about, would love the opportunity, dream role, excited to bring my experience.

**Empty self-assessment**: uniquely qualified, proven track record, results-driven, self-starter, detail-oriented, strong communicator, team player, thought leader, go-getter, hit the ground running.

**Corporate abstraction**: synergy, leverage (as a verb), utilize, drive value, move the needle, best-in-class, world-class, cutting-edge, robust (for anything other than software), impactful.

**Coach-writing constructions**: "the through-line in my career," "what sets me apart," "at the intersection of," "I bring a unique blend of," "my journey," "I am confident that my skills," "as someone who."

**AI-writing tells**: "In today's fast-paced world," "it is worth noting," "delve," "tapestry," "navigate the complexities," "not just X but Y," "a testament to," triads of adjectives, sentences that open with "Additionally" or "Furthermore," rhetorical questions followed by their own answer, and closing paragraphs that summarize the preceding paragraphs.

**Sincerity markers that undercut sincerity**: genuinely, honestly, truly, really (as intensifier), I believe that, I feel that.

**Structural tells**: em-dashes as the default connector; bulleted cover letters; a paragraph restating the JD; a paragraph restating the resume; any sentence that would survive the competitor-substitution test.

## 6. Weak versus strong

**Opening a cover letter**

Weak: "I am excited to apply for the Director of Technical Program Management position. With more than 15 years of experience leading complex programs and cross-functional teams, I believe my background aligns well with the requirements of the position."

Strong: "What caught my attention about this role is not the scope. It is the combination of technical program leadership, an engineering operating model still being defined, and a mandate to make cross-functional work predictable. That has been the recurring theme of my career, particularly where the technology was complex and the operating model was still evolving."

**Answering "why are you a fit"**

Weak: "I have strong communication, leadership, and program management skills."

Strong: "The strongest overlap is in three areas: leading technically complex programs, building the operating mechanisms around engineering delivery, and working across executives, engineers, and customers when the path is not obvious."

**Closing**

Weak: "I would be thrilled to bring my experience to your team and am confident I can make an immediate impact."

Strong: "Applying that experience to the platform underneath your core product is work I would find both technically interesting and consequential."

## 7. Worked example: "Why us?" before and after

Fictional candidate and company. Structure and edits are real; details are invented.

**Question**: "Why Halden Systems?" (Halden builds the telemetry platform that a fleet of industrial robots reports into; the role is a Staff TPM for the ingestion pipeline.)

**Draft, scored 7/10**

> The through-line in my career has been reliability. I have spent fifteen years building the operating discipline that keeps complex systems running, first at a payments processor and then at a logistics platform. As systems become more consequential, the reliability of the infrastructure underneath them matters more, not less. My experience spans distributed ingestion, on-call rotation, incident review, capacity planning, and vendor escalation. That is why Halden's mission resonates with me, and I would be excited to bring that experience to the ingestion team.

**What is wrong with it**

1. The question is "Why Halden?" and Halden does not appear until the second-to-last sentence. This is candidate-centric: career philosophy, then experience, then the company as a conclusion.
2. "The through-line in my career" is a coach-writing construction.
3. "As systems become more consequential..." is portable. Substitute any infrastructure company and it survives.
4. The capability list is the resume entering the answer.
5. The close is enthusiasm, not contribution.

**Rewrite**

> What draws me to Halden is that the ingestion pipeline is not a support system for the product. It is the product. If telemetry from a thousand robots arrives late or out of order, the fleet operators upstairs are making decisions on a picture that is already wrong, and Halden has been public about treating that as a safety problem rather than a data-quality problem.
>
> That framing is familiar. At the payments processor, I ran program delivery for the settlement pipeline, where a delayed batch was not a bug but a regulatory event. We built an internal replay capability to exercise the pipeline the way the busiest customers did, surface failure modes before they reached production, and give the on-call engineer something better than a pager and a prayer. I carried that rotation myself for two years.
>
> What I took from it is that operational readiness cannot be a gate at the end of development. It has to be part of how a system is designed, tested, released, and improved after something goes wrong.
>
> My best work has been where the architecture is still evolving, the stakes are high, and strong engineers need enough structure to make hard decisions quickly, without process becoming the objective. Building that structure around Halden's ingestion pipeline is work I would find both interesting and consequential.

**Why the rewrite is better**

It is not more polished. It is more decided. Halden is in the first sentence, and the stated reason is specific enough that it fails the competitor-substitution test in the right direction. The evidence explains why the interest is credible rather than serving as a resume summary. The reflection sentence shows an operating philosophy. The close names the work.

**Evidence note**: the replay capability and the on-call rotation must each carry an evidence level. If "carried that rotation for two years" is user-confirmed but not in the master (Level B), it is usable here and goes on the back-population queue. If it is an inference (Level C), the sentence is hedged or cut.

## 8. Pre-send checklist

- First sentence answers the question asked.
- On "Why us?", the employer appears in the first sentence.
- Every motivation sentence fails the competitor-substitution test (a competitor's name would not fit).
- Two or three proof points, each with mechanism and outcome; no capability lists.
- Every claim carries an evidence level; no Level D remains.
- Any obvious concern is named, with the transferring mechanism.
- Close is about contribution.
- Forbidden-phrase scan clean; em-dash scan clean.
- Reads in the candidate's own register.
- A hiring manager would think "this person understands what we are dealing with."
